Security of Retrieval-Augmented Generation Against
Targeted Knowledge-Base Poisoning
               An exploratory offline study of retrieval and extractive answer selection

Abstract
Targeted knowledge-base poisoning can place an attacker-selected false claim alongside relevant
evidence. This exploratory Afghanistan-focused study distinguishes supporting-evidence recovery,
malicious exposure, targeted answer corruption, and correct-answer restoration. The archived
principal experiment uses 224 documents, 225 chunks, 364 questions, 104 targets, and 21,840
correlated question-condition evaluations. Its actual pipeline comprises 1,024-dimensional hashing,
exact cosine retrieval, TF-IDF reranking, and deterministic sentence extraction; its numerical results
do not measure generative LLM behavior. A local audit recomputed response scores and replayed the
full grid with checksum-matched historical code. At full insertion, gold Recall@5 falls from 93.4% to
5.8%, lexical answer-value accuracy from 82.4% to 3.6%, and exposure-confirmed lexical attack
success reaches 73.1%. Reranking restores Recall@5 to 88.7% but leaves attack success at 72.1%; 73
targets retain gold evidence while producing the intended false value. Eligibility filtering prevents
rejected-label attacks. A separate target-specific accepted-label control yields 91/104 successes,
demonstrating dependence on metadata authority rather than implemented source authentication.
Separately archived neural retrieval controls and a 24-response generative development pilot
demonstrate implementation progress, not held-out generalization or human-adjudicated security
rates. The contribution is a bounded empirical analysis of distinct security outcomes. Fresh reference-
checked data, independent semantic review, and a frozen final generative experiment remain
necessary for broader claims.
Keywords: retrieval-augmented generation; knowledge-base poisoning; retrieval integrity; answer
integrity; provenance; adversarial evaluation

1. Introduction
Retrieval-augmented generation combines a language model with external evidence to support
knowledge-intensive tasks [11–13]. In institutional applications, retrieved material may include
public reports, administrative records, policy documents, or statistical observations. This access
makes the integrity of the knowledge base a security concern: a document can be highly relevant to a
question while supplying a false answer. A retrieval score consequently indicates relevance under a
scoring rule, not the factual reliability or authorized origin of the retrieved claim.
Prior research demonstrates that corpus insertions can influence downstream RAG responses [1–4].
The resulting security question extends beyond whether malicious text can be retrieved. A useful
assessment must distinguish whether supporting evidence remains available, whether attacker-
controlled material enters the answer context, and whether the answer adopts the intended false claim.
These outcomes can diverge. A defense that improves retrieval may preserve both correct and false
evidence, leaving the decision between them unresolved.

This study investigates those distinctions in an Afghanistan-focused factual corpus. The practical
implementation is the experimental instrument. The principal results describe an offline retrieval-and-
extraction pipeline whose deterministic behavior permits controlled comparisons. They must not be
attributed to generative language models. Appendix D separately records subsequent neural and
generative development checks; those checks do not replace the historical design or establish held-out
performance.
The research makes three bounded contributions. First, it evaluates supporting-evidence retrieval,
malicious exposure, answer-value correctness, and target-specific success within one controlled
design. Second, paired outcomes distinguish attack prevention from correctness restoration and newly
introduced failures. Third, separate repetition and forged-label controls identify the limits of duplicate
handling and metadata authority. The archived grid is accompanied by a local response-level audit
and re-execution. These contributions constitute an exploratory domain study and analytical
synthesis; no new attack algorithm, certified defense, or representative evaluation of deployed
generative RAG is claimed.
For institutions considering knowledge-based assistants, the resulting distinction is consequential:
recovering the correct source is an intermediate property, while producing a reliable answer is the
final objective. This paper examines that relationship and identifies the conditions under which the
studied controls offer protection.

2. Related research and study positioning

2.1. Retrieval augmentation and evidence use
RAG and dense passage retrieval establish the architectural basis for evidence-grounded question
answering [11, 12]. REPLUG extends retrieval augmentation to black-box language models [13].
These approaches motivate separate assessment of evidence selection and response construction.
Work on long-context use also shows that a language model's use of evidence can depend on its
placement [16]. The present extractive experiment does not reproduce those model-level effects; it
examines the more limited relationship between context membership and selected answer values.

2.2. Corpus poisoning and adjacent threats
PoisonedRAG studies targeted knowledge corruption through malicious knowledge-base insertions
[1]. CorruptRAG investigates practical attacks using a single poisoned text [2]. Su et al. examine
adversarial context and retrieval robustness [3], while a broader benchmark compares multiple
poisoning attacks and defenses [4]. These studies establish that targeted factual corruption is an
existing research problem. Our contribution concerns the interpretation of defense outcomes within a
bounded factual domain.
Attacker capabilities must be distinguished when relating results. PR-Attack coordinates poisoned
content with a prompt trigger [5], whereas the base attacker here controls corpus insertions only.
PANDORA studies poisoning for jailbreak objectives [6]. Indirect prompt injection uses external
content to convey instructions [14]; privacy attacks pursue information disclosure [7]; and
instruction-tuning poisoning changes training data [15]. These threats share an interest in untrusted
information but differ in access, intervention, and success criteria.

2.3. Defenses and outcome measurement
RobustRAG uses an isolate-then-aggregate strategy and develops guarantees under a defined
retrieval-corruption model [8]. RAGPart and RAGMask examine retrieval-stage defenses [9]. The

3. Analytical framework and research questions

3.1. Evidence availability, exposure, and answer integrity
Let K be the clean knowledge base and P_B a set of B inserted malicious passages. For query q and
defense d, the pipeline produces final context T_d(q, K ∪ P_B) and answer A_d(q). Let G(q) indicate
the annotated supporting chunk in that context, R(q) the target question's own malicious passage, C(q)
a lexical match to the expected value, and F(q) a lexical match to its designated false value. Let Z(q)
identify designated targets. Matching indicators measure text containment under normalization and
token boundaries; they do not establish semantic correctness.
The scored answer-value correctness and targeted-success indicators are:
                    V(q) = C(q) × [1 − F(q)];            S(q) = Z(q) × R(q) × F(q) × [1 − C(q)].

For questions without an assigned false value, F(q) is zero. An answer matching both values is
flagged as ambiguous and receives neither correctness credit nor attack-success credit. No such
ambiguous answers occur in the archived main run, but the exclusion remains part of the scoring rule.

Mixed or negated claims can still be unsafe despite this conservative convention. Target-specific
retrieval is necessary for credited attack success; another question's poison does not satisfy that
requirement.
The central analytical distinction follows from the pipeline: G(q) = 1 does not imply R(q) = 0, and
neither context membership indicator determines the output value. Correct and malicious passages
can coexist. Thus high gold recall cannot, on its own, establish resistance to targeted manipulation.
Conversely, eliminating malicious context can restore clean behavior without correcting errors
already present in the clean answerer.

3.2. Attack coverage and interpretation of dose curves
Let I_B(q) indicate whether the passage targeting q is inserted. The shared-corpus design inserts one
passage per selected target, and the scored outcome requires retrieval of that target's own passage.
Therefore S(q) ≤ I_B(q). With 104 designated targets and B distinct target passages, the all-target
success rate satisfies:
                                ASR_all(B) = Σ S(q) / 104 ≤ B / 104.

This is a counting bound derived from the experiment's success definition, not a robustness guarantee.
At B = 10, all-target ASR cannot exceed approximately 9.6% under this design, even if every inserted
attack succeeds. An increasing ASR curve can consequently reflect wider coverage as well as changes
in retrieval competition. To isolate per-target strength, the target set must remain fixed while attack
multiplicity or diversity changes. The supplementary repetition experiment addresses multiplicity
under a different protocol; it is analyzed separately.

3.3. Research questions and exploratory expectations
RQ1 examines how poisoning changes supporting-evidence retrieval and malicious exposure. RQ2
examines the relationship between exposure, answer-value correctness, and intended false answers.
RQ3 examines the protection provided by reranking, trusted-label filtering, and their combination.
RQ4 examines the corresponding clean-performance cost.
Four exploratory expectations are examined within the historical design: H2, poisoning is associated
with increased incorrect or manipulated answers; H3, reranking reduces malicious rank or inclusion;
H4, filtering with reranking provides greater protection than reranking alone; and H5, defenses may
impose a clean-performance cost. Their original identifiers are retained for traceability; these
expectations were not preregistered. H1, an advantage of targeted over matched unrelated poison, is
an untested prospective question because the required comparison condition is absent. It is excluded
from claims of empirical support.

3.4. Attacker capabilities and trusted authority
The base attacker inserts passages into a separate copy of the clean corpus. Each passage associates a
designated question with a selected false value. Existing clean text, retrieval and answerer code, and
defense implementation remain fixed. Prompt control, training-data modification, and production-
system compromise are outside this base condition. The study does not establish performance against
adaptive attack optimization.
The metadata condition assumes that an ingestion authority assigns reliable trust and verification
labels. All 104 base attack entries carry rejected labels: untrusted and unverified. Of the 225 clean
chunks, 223 are eligible; two inherited mineral-resource chunks are verified but have low trust and
are rejected. The filter implements an eligibility policy over supplied labels, without independently

4.1. Corpus and reference questions
The corpus combines 56 inherited public-information documents and 168 World Development
Indicators observations across 12 Afghanistan indicator families for 2010–2023 [10]. It contains 224
documents and 225 chunks. The question set contains 28 inherited questions and 336 indicator
questions, with two formulations for each indicator-year observation. There are 104 designated
targets and one original malicious passage per target. Table 2 summarizes the composition.

4.2. Executed retrieval-and-extraction pipeline
The principal experiment is the archived run of 21 September 2026, 20260921T061151Z_d1966106.
Its manifest, response records, configuration, and checksum-matched source identify 1,024-
dimensional token hashing, deterministic exact cosine search, a query-and-candidate TF-IDF
reranker, and an extractive-stub answerer. The retriever produces 30 candidates; the final context
contains at most five passages. The historical answerer scans sentences in context order and selects
the sentence with greatest query-keyword overlap, retaining the first sentence on ties. It excludes a
fixed stopword list, including Afghanistan, and does not reason about claim entailment.
The extractive backend ignores the configured system prompt, temperature, and maximum output-
token parameter. Their presence in a configuration file is consequently not evidence of instruction-
following protection or controlled LLM generation. Neural embedding and cross-encoder model
names also appear in configuration fields, but the recorded active backends are hashing and TF-IDF.
Later lexical/evidence revisions change both ranking and sentence selection; Appendix B documents
their separate diagnostic.

4.3. Experimental comparisons
Each of 364 questions is evaluated at insertion budgets 0, 10, 30, 60, and 104, under four defense
settings and three attack-selection seeds (17, 42, 91): 364 × 5 × 4 × 3 = 21,840 evaluations. The
corresponding inserted shares are 0.0%, 4.3%, 11.8%, 21.1%, and 31.6%, calculated as B/(225 + B).
Each malicious passage is indexed as one preconstructed chunk; the budgets count passages rather
than independently sampled documents or sources. A seeded permutation selects nested subsets at
intermediate budgets. At full insertion, 104/225 = 46.2% is the amount added relative to the clean
corpus, whereas 104/329 = 31.6% is the malicious share of the resulting corpus. This severe stress
condition does not estimate institutional prevalence or performance at 1% contamination.
Seeds select reproducible subsets from the fixed attack pool. They do not sample independent
generated responses. At zero and full budget, selections are identical across seeds. Intermediate
budgets change both target coverage and shared-corpus competition, as explained in Section 3.2.

4.4. Outcomes and denominators
Gold Recall@5 is the fraction of all 364 questions whose single annotated supporting chunk appears
in the final context. MRR averages its reciprocal final-context rank, with zero when absent. These
annotations may omit alternative legitimate support. Answer-value accuracy averages V(q) over the
same 364 questions. Target-specific exposure and all-target ASR use the 104 designated targets. ASR
among injected targets uses only questions whose own attack is present in the KB: the denominator
equals B at each positive budget in this all-question protocol. It is undefined at B = 0 because no
target has an inserted attack. All-designated-target ASR is zero at that budget under the exposure-
confirmed rule; that zero is not evidence of protection. Any-poison exposure uses all 364 questions;
malicious occupancy is the fraction of final context slots containing any inserted poison.
Answer-value scoring lowercases and normalizes text, strips selected hedge prefixes and parenthetical
material from reference values, removes commas and currency symbols, and uses token boundaries. It
excludes simultaneous expected/false-value matches from accuracy. Strict normalized exact match
and token F1 are auxiliary measures. Value containment cannot reliably distinguish negation,
quotation, the wrong entity, the wrong year, or incompatible units. The metric is therefore lexical
answer-value accuracy, not fully adjudicated factual or semantic accuracy.
For defense d, let U be the targets with successful undefended attacks at a fixed budget. Paired
prevention counts targets in U whose defended attacks no longer succeed, divided by |U|. Correctness
restoration counts targets in U with correct defended answers, using the same denominator. Newly
successful attacks count targets outside U whose defended outputs become scored successes. Clean
cost is the clean accuracy without defense minus clean accuracy with defense, in percentage points.
These quantities describe different transitions and should not be substituted for one another.

4.5. Analysis and supplementary protocol
Across-seed means and sample standard deviations describe sensitivity to attack selection. At zero
and full budget, the attack sets, contexts, and answers are identical across seeds. These repeated rows
are not independent samples, and no confirmatory p-values or population confidence intervals are
claimed. The archived software exports descriptive Wilson intervals; the article does not interpret
them as inferential uncertainty. Figures are generated from unrounded per-question proportions rather
than from rounded summary percentages.
Evidence verification used a separate analysis script to check archived output hashes, current input
hashes, question-to-reference links, attack selections, retrieved text and metadata, and all five lexical
verdict fields. All 21,840 main records were rescored without disagreement. The full grid was then

5. Results

5.1. Supporting evidence and malicious exposure
At full insertion, undefended gold Recall@5 falls from 93.4% to 5.8%, while MRR falls from 0.837
to 0.045. The target's own poison appears in the final context for 97 of 104 targets. Any poison
appears for 97.5% of all questions, and malicious passages occupy 93.0% of final context slots on
average. Poisoning therefore affects both directly targeted questions and the broader availability of
useful evidence.
Reranking restores full-budget gold Recall@5 to 88.7% and MRR to 0.637, yet own-poison exposure
rises from 97 to 98 of 104 targets. In 73 target questions, the final reranked context contains the gold
chunk and the target poison while the answer meets the false-only attack-success rule. This observed
coexistence directly demonstrates that evidence recovery does not establish answer integrity. H1
remains untested because there is no matched unrelated-poison condition.

the 76 initially successful attacks receives a correct defended answer. Paired prevention is 2.6%;
correctness restoration is 1.3%. Table 5 retains these transitions rather than representing the net ASR

Filtering preserves clean answer-value accuracy at 82.4%; filtering then reranking preserves the

5.5. Repetition and forged-label controls
In the separate target-specific repetition experiment, undefended successes are 90/104 with one
malicious copy and 91/104 with two or more. All 104 answer strings remain unchanged between five,
ten, twenty, and fifty copies. The plateau is consistent with saturation of a five-slot context; it does
not imply that nominal insertion counts determine continuously worsening answers.
Reranked successes increase from 37/104 with one copy to 61/104 with two and 92/104 with five or
more. Exact duplicate collapse retains 90/104 successes at every positive nominal dose: it reduces
identical multiplicity to one effective passage without making that passage reliable. The intervention
concerns exact repeated text, not diverse or semantically similar poisons.
At 50 identical copies per target, filtering yields 0/104 scored successes under rejected metadata;
changing only those entries to high trust and verified yields 91/104 successes (87.5%) in the matched
target-specific control. Each target has its own KB, rather than 5,200 copies sharing one collection.
This result identifies dependence on metadata authority under changed attacker capability; it is not a
direct comparison with the main shared-KB 76/104 result or an implemented authentication test. Only
saved-response rescoring, not a new replay of this supplementary experiment, was performed. Table 6
keeps the protocols separate.

6. Discussion and institutional implications

6.1. Recovering evidence is an intermediate security outcome
The principal finding is a separation between evidence recovery and answer integrity. Reranking can
recover the annotated supporting passage while keeping the malicious passage in context. Its large
Recall@5 improvement would therefore overstate protection if considered without targeted outputs.
Overall answer improvement also masks persistent vulnerability among the attacked targets.
Conversely, zero scored attacks after filtering does not imply perfect correctness, because clean
answer errors remain.
These distinctions support reporting a sequence of outcomes: supporting-evidence availability,
malicious exposure, intended false output, and correct-answer restoration. No single measure captures
the sequence. This reporting approach is especially useful when evaluating an assistant whose sources
may contain competing assertions rather than explicit malicious instructions.

6.2. Trust labels represent governance assumptions
The eligibility policy is effective because its accepted/rejected labels separate malicious insertions
from useful clean material. Its security boundary is therefore the authority that assigns and protects
those labels. A trusted source may still be outdated or factually wrong, and compromised accepted
metadata changes the threat model. Source authentication and claim validation address related but
distinct problems.
The forged-label control motivates careful accounting of where trust enters a pipeline. A provenance
gate should be described by its authority, verification process, update rules, and attacker access, rather
than by a label name alone. The present experiment measures neither authentication implementation
nor the real-world cost of source review.

6.3. Implications for institutional knowledge management
For prospective institutional use, the results support evaluating source governance and answer
integrity alongside retrieval quality. Table 7 presents proposed actions tied to the observed
mechanisms. These recommendations do not constitute ministry approval, evidence of deployment, or
measured operational effectiveness. An institutional pilot would need accountable ownership of
ingestion decisions, verifiable changes to source and trust metadata, and an answer-review process
suitable for the actual user tasks.

7. Validity limitations and future research
7.1. Scope of the empirical evidence
The principal factorial study contains no generative LLM inference. Hashing and deterministic
extraction do not capture neural retrieval, parametric knowledge, instruction following, stochastic
generation or semantic handling of conflicting evidence. The historical answerer ignores the
configured system prompt. Its numerical findings characterize that pipeline. Subsequent neural and
generative development checks in Appendix D have distinct protocols and no independent human
adjudication; they do not establish the original ASR for an LLM.
The small corpus is dominated by templated statistical observations. Correlated years and question
formulations limit independent-sample interpretation, and inherited references require further
checking. A single annotated gold chunk can omit alternative support. All existing splits were
inspected, preventing claims of held-out generalization. The severe full-pool contamination rate and
the absence of adaptive attack optimization also limit external validity.

7.2. Measurement and comparison limitations
Lexical scoring may misclassify negation, quotation, temporal mismatches, and mixed explanations.
The success definition confirms a false-only lexical outcome with target-specific exposure; it is not a
complete semantic safety measure. Strict exact match is poorly aligned with the answer format.
Future evaluation should distinguish correct answers, intended false answers, other errors, conflicting
claims, abstentions, and execution failures.
The dose curve combines target coverage with competition. Repeated-copy and shared-corpus
experiments differ in their surrounding attack sets and must not be pooled. The present study does not
independently identify effects of corpus size, context length, attack diversity, or correct-evidence
density. Across-seed SD measures selection sensitivity; zero endpoint variation is not population
certainty. No untouched test set, external replication, or permanent artifact identifier is claimed.

7.3. Research continuation
The final generative evaluation must freeze retriever and generator revisions, prompts, generation
parameters, defense order, attack protocol and scoring rules before test outcomes are inspected. The
neural components and generator have passed separate development integration checks (Appendix

D), but fresh data and independent semantic assessment remain outstanding. Failures must stop
execution or create separately labelled conditions, without silent model substitution. Partition fresh
questions by underlying fact and linked sources; keep related formulations together.
Matched unrelated poison and benign insertions are needed to test H1 and separate targeted effects
from generic index competition. A fixed-target design should report insertion coverage, exposure
among attacked targets, and success conditional on exposure. The pre-retrieval gate should be
compared with a separately specified rerank-then-filter condition, including context backfilling and
false rejection of legitimate documents.
Blinded human adjudication should check intended false claims and factual correctness, with
independent second review and documented disagreement resolution. Numeric answers require entity,
relation, year, units, and tolerance. Uncertainty should account for shared facts and source families
rather than treating deterministic repetitions as independent samples. An additional QA dataset and
additional generative models would strengthen external validity, but are extensions beyond the
completed pilot.

8. Conclusion
This research analyzes targeted knowledge-base poisoning through a controlled offline study
supported by prior literature and verified local evidence. Retrieval recovery, attack prevention, and
correctness restoration are distinct outcomes: reranking recovers much of the annotated evidence
while leaving most full-budget target manipulations successful. An authoritative metadata gate
prevents rejected-label attacks, but accepted-label forgery removes that protection in a separate
control. Repeated-copy experiments show that nominal insertion volume can exceed the effective
five-passage context without changing the answer.
The study offers a bounded empirical basis for institutional evaluation of knowledge integrity. Its
principal response-level results were recomputed and locally reproduced with checksum-matched
historical code. That verification strengthens reproducibility within the pilot; it does not establish
semantic correctness, independent-source validation of inherited facts, or general robustness of
generative RAG. Further scientific claims should follow fresh evaluation data, blinded adjudication,
and explicitly versioned neural and generative experiments.

Appendix A. Relationship to the original research plan
The original proposal document was not supplied for independent inspection. The following
comparison therefore concerns the research plan as described in earlier manuscripts, rather than
confirmed compliance with a separately reviewed proposal. That description anticipated
approximately 100-150 documents, 500-1,500 chunks, 200-300 questions, and 50 targets. The
executed corpus instead contains 224 documents, 225 chunks, 364 questions, and 104 targets. Many
records are short statistical observations, so document count does not measure linguistic diversity.
The described plan involved ChromaDB, neural embeddings, a fixed generative LLM, and filtering
after reranking. The verified principal run instead uses exact cosine search, token hashing,
deterministic extraction, and eligibility before retrieval. Its budgets are 0, 10, 30, 60, and 104,
compared with described planned budgets of 0, 5, 10, and 20. Current application defaults use 0, 10,
20, 50, and 104 and must not replace the principal run's configuration. These are disclosed
implementation and protocol deviations; the planned neural and generative components were not
executed in the principal study. Subsequent development checks are reported separately in Appendix
D.
